# Netherlands / Amsterdam–Rotterdam Reuse Bubble

**Working focus:** reclaimed building materials, urban mining, material passports, circular construction platforms, and the architectural culture that made reuse visible in the Netherlands.

**Version:** evidence-backed combined research file  
**Prepared:** 2026-06-06  
**Geographic frame:** Netherlands, with a strong Amsterdam–Rotterdam axis and related case studies in Leiden, Eindhoven, Enschede, and national platforms.

---

## 0. Research position

The Dutch reuse bubble is best understood as an **urban-mining and circular-design ecosystem** rather than a single organization. Its strongest infrastructural logic is the connection between:

1. **Design practice** — especially Superuse Studios and its method of harvesting, mapping, and designing from available residual materials.[^superuse-harvest]
2. **Material visibility and marketplaces** — Oogstkaart/Harvest Map, New Horizon, Insert, Madopt/Repurpose, and other Dutch exchange platforms.[^superuse-oogst-newhorizon][^insert-marketplace][^madopt]
3. **Urban mining implementation** — New Horizon’s building scans, material inventories, donor-building logic, and Urban Mining Collective.[^newhorizon-home][^newhorizon-harvest][^newhorizon-donor][^circo-newhorizon]
4. **Data and material passports** — Madaster and Platform CB’23’s passport guidance, which turn buildings into documented future material banks.[^madaster-material-passport][^madaster-position][^platformcb23]
5. **Policy and city strategy** — national 2050 circular-economy policy and Amsterdam’s circular-economy strategy, especially for the built environment.[^gov-2050][^amsterdam-circular]
6. **Demonstration buildings** — projects such as Villa Welpeloo, Wikado/Blade Made, BlueCity Offices, Buitenplaats Brienenoord, De Ceuvel, Circl, People’s Pavilion, and BioPartner 5 show how the culture translated into built work.[^villa-welpeloo][^wikado][^bluecity][^buitenplaats][^deceuvel][^circl][^peoples][^biopartner]

The ecosystem is infrastructural because it attempts to make reuse **findable, specifiable, insurable, documentable, purchasable, and repeatable** — not only poetic or one-off.

---

## 1. Short thesis

The Netherlands reuse bubble is a **design-to-market-to-policy loop**:

```text
Superuse Studios / 2012Architecten
        ↓
Harvesting method + Oogstkaart / Harvest Map
        ↓
New Horizon Urban Mining + Urban Mining Collective
        ↓
Material scans, donor buildings, circular products, matching demand and supply
        ↓
Market/data layer: Insert, Madopt, Madaster, CB'23 passports
        ↓
Municipal/national policy: Amsterdam circular strategy + Dutch 2050 circular economy target
        ↓
Built precedents: Circl, BioPartner 5, BlueCity, De Ceuvel, People’s Pavilion, Villa Welpeloo, Wikado
```

**Core interpretation:** the Dutch bubble is less about one physical salvage depot than about **making material flows visible and actionable** through mapping, urban mining, platforms, standards, and demonstration architecture.

## 3. Main actors and their network roles

### 3.1 Superuse Studios — design practice and method engine

**Role:** architectural practice and methodological origin point for Dutch material-harvesting design culture.

Superuse Studios describes “harvesting” as the act of searching, finding, and dismantling reusable building materials.[^superuse-harvest] It states that it founded **Oogstkaart.nl** in 2012 as a marketplace for reusable building materials, used by Superuse and by external architects, design professionals, builders, and project developers.[^superuse-harvest] Superuse also says Oogstkaart was sold to New Horizon in 2019, and that New Horizon continued the platform from 2020.[^superuse-oogst-newhorizon]

**Why this is infrastructural:** Superuse turned reuse from an aesthetic choice into a repeatable workflow: scout, map, harvest, adapt design, build, and document. The important object is not only a building; it is the **method of designing from available secondary resources**.

**Evidence confidence:** A+ for Superuse’s own methodology and Oogstkaart claims.

---

### 3.2 Oogstkaart / Harvest Map — material visibility infrastructure

**Role:** early Dutch platform for making secondary material sources visible to designers and builders.

Superuse states that Oogstkaart was developed in 2012 to improve the findability of secondary material sources and to make the supply of discarded components and elements transparent to designers and builders.[^superuse-oogst-newhorizon] Superuse also states that the platform was sold to New Horizon, while Superuse would continue to provide materialization advice.[^superuse-oogst-newhorizon]

**Why this is infrastructural:** Oogstkaart shifts reuse from opportunistic searching to **searchable supply visibility**. Even if specific platform ownership and activity have changed, the concept is foundational for Dutch reuse culture.

**Evidence confidence:** A+ for origin and transfer; interpretation for the broader importance.

---

### 3.3 New Horizon Urban Mining — implementation and supply-chain actor

**Role:** urban mining company, building-scan and material-inventory actor, and network organizer.

New Horizon’s own site says it believes reuse of construction materials addresses economic and ecological challenges, and that it connects data, knowledge, and chain partners to make reuse measurable, executable, and profitable.[^newhorizon-home] New Horizon describes urban mining as dismantling buildings with the aim of harvesting as many materials as possible for reuse.[^newhorizon-harvest] It also explicitly seeks donor buildings at scale to feed production and distribution of circular building materials.[^newhorizon-donor]

CIRCO’s New Horizon case describes urban mining as recovering building materials and products from existing buildings. It says New Horizon positions itself not as a demolisher but as a material supplier that mines the built environment as a source of raw materials.[^circo-newhorizon] The same CIRCO case says New Horizon works with other companies in an **Urban Mining Collective** to give construction materials a new destination while retaining value.[^circo-newhorizon]

**Why this is infrastructural:** New Horizon turns donor buildings into supply streams and connects demolition, material inventory, productization, distribution, and demand.

**Evidence confidence:** A+ for official claims; A for CIRCO case. Some impact claims are self-reported or partner-reported and should be treated carefully.

---

### 3.4 Urban Mining Collective / Oogst Collectief — network layer around New Horizon

**Role:** chain collaboration and market-building structure.

New Horizon describes its Oogst Collectief as a network intended to connect material demand and supply, provide knowledge and inspiration sessions, and position members as preferred suppliers within related company structures.[^newhorizon-collective] CIRCO describes the Urban Mining Collective as a network of organizations working together to give construction materials a new destination while retaining value.[^circo-newhorizon]

**Why this is infrastructural:** it addresses a key problem in reclaimed construction: reuse requires more than inventory; it needs producers, demolition contractors, distributors, architects, clients, and builders to coordinate.

**Evidence confidence:** A+ for New Horizon’s own description; A for CIRCO description; interpretation for systemic role.

---

### 3.5 Madaster — material passport and data infrastructure

**Role:** digital registry and material-passport platform for circular real estate and infrastructure.

Madaster describes a material passport as a digital record of the composition and reuse potential of materials within a building.[^madaster-material-passport] Madaster describes its platform as registering materials and products used in buildings and infrastructure, generating material passports, calculating circularity-related indicators, and showing salvage value.[^madaster-position]

A European Circular Economy Stakeholder Platform entry describes Madaster as an online registry intended to enable and promote circularity in construction and eliminate waste. It states that data are recorded on components used in real estate and infrastructure projects, including information on dismantling, reuse, embodied carbon, and toxicity.[^madaster-eu]

Madaster’s position paper states that Madaster started in 2017 in the Netherlands and is active in multiple European countries.[^madaster-position]

**Why this is infrastructural:** material passports provide the data layer that lets a future owner know what exists, where it is, what it is made of, and whether it can be recovered.

**Evidence confidence:** A+ for platform functions and origin; A for EU stakeholder-platform description.

---

### 3.6 Platform CB’23 — standardization and shared language

**Role:** Dutch sector-wide circular-construction guidance and agreement-making platform.

Platform CB’23 says it made national, industry-wide agreements on circular construction and developed seven guidelines and two application tools between 2018 and 2023.[^platformcb23] It says the work involved professionals from different parts of the Dutch construction sector, covering both buildings and civil engineering.[^platformcb23]

**Why this is infrastructural:** reuse cannot scale through examples alone. It needs shared definitions, measuring methods, design guidance, procurement guidance, passport guidance, and agreement frameworks.

**Evidence confidence:** A+.

---

### 3.7 Insert — circular marketplace for construction, public space, and green-sector materials

**Role:** national marketplace and services platform for used building materials, public-space materials, and green objects.

Insert describes its marketplace as a platform for offering used materials from construction, outdoor space, and green objects.[^insert-marketplace] It states that, with dozens of suppliers, 250 project locations, and thousands of reusable materials, Insert has grown into the largest circular platform in the Netherlands for the construction, civil, and green sectors.[^insert-marketplace]

Insert also provides material-inventory services, noting that inventories are essential when used construction materials are to be sold.[^insert-services]

**Why this is infrastructural:** Insert represents the marketplace and inventory layer needed to move components from demolition or surplus into new projects.

**Evidence confidence:** A+ for platform self-description; caveat: claims about being “largest” are self-reported.

---

### 3.8 Madopt / Repurpose — demand-driven reuse platform and consultancy

**Role:** demand-driven marketplace and reuse consultancy.

Madopt states that it was developed by Repurpose BV, a reuse advisory and scouting company, and that Repurpose has experience organizing reuse of more than 100,000 building materials.[^madopt] Madopt explains that the idea grew from the observation that demand-driven work is more effective than supply-driven work.[^madopt-demand]

Repurpose describes itself as enabling building with reuse and building for reuse, offering masterclasses, building scans, and the Madopt marketplace.[^repurpose]

**Why this is infrastructural:** Madopt is important because it reverses the common marketplace logic: instead of only listing available materials, it lets buyers express demand so suppliers can respond.

**Evidence confidence:** A+ for platform claims; caveat: material-number claims are self-reported.

---

### 3.9 Cirkelstad and Het Nieuwe Normaal — network and norm-setting layer

**Role:** city/network platform and circular-construction standardization effort.

Holland Circular Hotspot describes Cirkelstad as a concept that started with waste materials used in Rotterdam for new construction and renovation projects and was later rolled out nationally, with cities such as Rotterdam, Amsterdam, Utrecht, and Amersfoort adopting the concept.[^cirkelstad]

Het Nieuwe Normaal describes itself as a shared standard and language for circular construction. Its guide says HNN began as an initiative of Cirkelstad and the Dutch Ministry of the Interior and Kingdom Relations, with cooperation between major clients and contractors.[^hnn]

**Why this is infrastructural:** Cirkelstad/HNN help normalize circular construction by creating shared criteria, common language, and peer learning across clients and contractors.

**Evidence confidence:** A for Cirkelstad summary; A for HNN guide.

---

### 3.10 Amsterdam and national policy layer

**Role:** policy environment that makes circular construction a public-sector priority.

The Dutch Government states that by 2050 the Netherlands should have a fully circular economy, meaning products and raw materials are constantly reused and hardly anything is discarded as waste.[^gov-2050] The same government page notes that agreements with the construction industry include reusing construction waste and materials from demolished buildings and using more sustainable biobased materials.[^gov-2050]

Amsterdam’s circular-economy page identifies the built environment as one of its action areas and lists actions including using natural materials in construction, requiring reuse in projects, applying circular design in new developments, renovating municipal property sustainably, and making reuse standard in public space.[^amsterdam-circular]

**Why this is infrastructural:** policy creates demand and legitimizes reuse through public procurement, municipal property, public-space work, and city-wide circular targets.

**Evidence confidence:** A+.

## 4. Buildings and case studies within the Dutch bubble

### 4.1 Villa Welpeloo — Enschede, Superuse Studios

**Type:** private house / early flagship of reclaimed-material design.  
**Network role:** proof that design can begin from harvested local material streams.

Superuse describes Villa Welpeloo as a residential house in Enschede for which scouts investigated the availability of materials in the surrounding area; the design then developed from the found materials.[^villa-welpeloo] The Ellen MacArthur Foundation case says Villa Welpeloo was constructed from 60% salvaged material and that Google Earth helped select the construction area based on accessibility to industrial waste materials.[^emf-villa]

**Infrastructural reading:** Villa Welpeloo is not itself infrastructure, but it is an early demonstration of the “harvest first, design second” method that later became infrastructural through Oogstkaart and Superuse’s wider practice.

**Evidence confidence:** A+ for Superuse project description; A for Ellen MacArthur Foundation percentage.

---

### 4.2 Wikado / Blade Made — Rotterdam, Superuse Studios

**Type:** playground / outdoor reuse of wind-turbine blades.  
**Network role:** material-stream prototype for end-of-life rotor blades.

Superuse’s Blade Made page says the Wikado project began when designing a playground for Kinderparadijs Meidoorn in Rotterdam, using five decommissioned wind-turbine blades and reusable elements from the previous playground.[^wikado] A peer-reviewed Sustainability article describes Wikado as a Rotterdam playground built out of reused blades from a dismantled wind turbine and designed by Superuse Studios.[^wikado-mdpi]

**Infrastructural reading:** Wikado is important because it identifies a recurring future material stream — decommissioned rotor blades — and tests how it can become a construction/playground element.

**Evidence confidence:** A+ for Superuse project page; A for peer-reviewed source.

---

### 4.3 BlueCity Offices — Rotterdam, Superuse Studios / BlueCity

**Type:** adaptive reuse / circular office and innovation ecosystem.  
**Network role:** links reuse architecture to a broader circular-economy community in Rotterdam.

Superuse states that for BlueCity Offices the main material input was reused window frames as partition walls, followed by reused steel, with European structural timber and conventional hinges/locks used where needed.[^bluecity] Superuse reports a 68% CO2 reduction compared with conventional office refurbishment.[^bluecity] Circle Economy describes BlueCity as the repurposing of the former Tropicana water park into a circular model city and says the project achieved high material reuse and created space for sustainable startups.[^bluecity-circle]

**Infrastructural reading:** BlueCity combines building reuse, component reuse, and a tenant ecosystem devoted to circular entrepreneurship.

**Evidence confidence:** A+ for Superuse material claim and CO2 claim; B/A for Circle Economy summary. CO2 and percentage claims should be treated as project-reported unless independently audited.

---

### 4.4 Buitenplaats Brienenoord — Rotterdam, Superuse Studios

**Type:** cultural/community building.  
**Network role:** on-site building reuse + harvested material map + community construction.

Superuse states that the project reused the materials and foundations of the old Kamphuis as much as possible, and that 90% of the old building was reused.[^buitenplaats] Superuse also states that, except for fasteners, five wooden trusses, column boots, and glazing on the south façade, everything was made from materials with a previous life.[^buitenplaats]

A Superuse exhibition text explains that 90% of materials came from demolition of the on-site scouting building — including foundation, trusses, window frames, and woodwork — and that other materials were harvested at distances ranging from 2.5 km to 140 km.[^superuse-harvest]

**Infrastructural reading:** the project demonstrates a repeatable logistics/design strategy for reusing a donor building on its own site, especially under access and budget constraints.

**Evidence confidence:** A+.

---

### 4.5 De Ceuvel — Amsterdam North, Space&Matter / Metabolic / partners

**Type:** circular urban living lab and creative workspace.  
**Network role:** urban regeneration, reused houseboats, cleantech, community and education.

De Ceuvel’s own sustainability page states that De Ceuvel was built largely out of recycled materials, with old houseboats that would have been demolished upcycled into creative, energy-efficient workspaces using secondhand materials from across the Netherlands.[^deceuvel] Metabolic describes De Ceuvel as a project that used 17 old houseboats, specialized plants, and low-cost technologies to turn a polluted shipyard into a regenerative urban oasis.[^metabolic-deceuvel]

**Infrastructural reading:** De Ceuvel is not simply a building. It is an urban lab connecting adaptive reuse, reclaimed materials, site remediation, energy/water experimentation, and public learning.

**Evidence confidence:** A+ for De Ceuvel and Metabolic descriptions.

---

### 4.6 Circl Pavilion — Amsterdam Zuidas, ABN AMRO / de Architekten Cie / BAM / partners

**Type:** circular pavilion, later dismantling test case.  
**Network role:** material passport, design for disassembly, component take-back, circular deconstruction.

ABN AMRO announced Circl as a circular pavilion and described circularity as materials being reused as much as possible within their maximum lifespan.[^circl-abn] ABN AMRO also states that the idea of material passports inspired the development of a materials passport for Circl.[^madaster-abn] One Planet Network says the whole Circl building was designed to be dismantled and, in theory, materials can be recovered close to their original form.[^circl-oneplanet]

The Zuidas redevelopment site reported in 2024 that Circl’s dismantling aimed to harvest as much construction material as possible for reuse, and cited a target of reusing around 70% of the building.[^circl-zuidas] DERIX, the timber supplier, later described the Circl dismantling as a use case for circular timber construction and reported that inspected timber components were in excellent condition and already had plans for reuse in other buildings.[^circl-derix]

**Infrastructural reading:** Circl is crucial because it does not stop at design for disassembly; its later dismantling tests whether circular design can actually return components to use.

**Evidence confidence:** A+ for ABN AMRO, One Planet, Zuidas, and DERIX claims. The 70% figure is an aim/target reported during deconstruction, not necessarily final verified reuse.

---

### 4.7 People’s Pavilion — Eindhoven, bureau SLA + Overtreders W / Arup

**Type:** temporary pavilion for Dutch Design Week 2017.  
**Network role:** radical borrowed-material construction and return logistics.

Bureau SLA states that People’s Pavilion was a 250 m2 temporary building made from 100% borrowed materials, including concrete and wooden beams, lighting, façade elements, glass roof, and recycled plastic cladding, which were returned unharmed after Dutch Design Week except for the plastic cladding tiles distributed to residents.[^peoples] Overtreders W states that materials were borrowed for nine days and returned to owners after Dutch Design Week, with no screws, glue, drills, or saws used on the construction site; materials were strapped together instead.[^peoples-overtreders]

Arup states that it delivered structural design and that borrowed materials were returned after dismantling; many items were reused in further construction projects.[^peoples-arup]

**Infrastructural reading:** People’s Pavilion demonstrates a short-life building model based on borrowing, reversible assembly, legal/contractual return, and temporary-use circularity.

**Evidence confidence:** A+ for designer and engineer sources.

---

### 4.8 BioPartner 5 — Leiden Bio Science Park, PTSA / NIBE / partners

**Type:** laboratory/incubator building.  
**Network role:** serious structural steel reuse and high-performance building reuse case.

BioPartner Leiden states that BioPartner 5 is characterized by large-scale material reuse, ranging from 160 tons of donor steel to furniture and doors.[^biopartner] Leiden Bio Science Park states that around 150 tons of steel from the former Gorlaeus high-rise building were reused in BioPartner 5’s main structure, and that bricks, slate, sewage pipes, wood, and furniture were given a second life in the building.[^leiden-circularity]

Dutch Design Awards states that steel construction, natural stone, and rubble from a demolished neighbouring laboratory were reused, and that the project shows how construction materials can be given a high-quality second life.[^biopartner-dda]

**Infrastructural reading:** BioPartner 5 is important because it moves reuse into the structural layer of a demanding laboratory/incubator building, not only interiors or pavilions.

**Evidence confidence:** A+ for BioPartner/Leiden Bio Science Park; A for Dutch Design Awards.

## 6. What makes this bubble infrastructural?

### 6.1 It has a language for reuse

The Dutch bubble has an unusually explicit vocabulary: **harvesting**, **donor buildings**, **urban mining**, **material passports**, **building as material bank**, **circular construction**, **design for disassembly**, and **reuse marketplaces**. The vocabulary matters because it lets clients, designers, demolition contractors, suppliers, and policymakers coordinate.

**Evidence basis:** Superuse uses “harvesting” and Oogstkaart; New Horizon uses “donor buildings” and urban mining; Madaster uses “material passport”; Platform CB’23 establishes shared sector agreements.[^superuse-harvest][^newhorizon-donor][^madaster-material-passport][^platformcb23]

---

### 6.2 It connects design with demolition

The Dutch ecosystem repeatedly links architects and designers with demolition/urban-mining actors. Superuse’s Oogstkaart became part of New Horizon; CIRCO frames New Horizon as a material supplier rather than a demolisher; TU Delft research explicitly studies collaboration between demolition companies and architects.[^superuse-oogst-newhorizon][^circo-newhorizon][^tudelft-demolition-architect]

**Interpretation:** this connection is one of the strongest signs of infrastructure. Reuse becomes scalable only when demolition is no longer the end of the chain and design is no longer limited to new-product catalogs.

---

### 6.3 It includes market tools, not just showcase buildings

Insert, Madopt, Madaster, Oogstkaart, and New Horizon all address the same problem from different angles: **materials must be visible, documented, valued, and matched to demand**.[^insert-marketplace][^madopt][^madaster-position][^newhorizon-home]

**Interpretation:** buildings like Villa Welpeloo or BioPartner 5 demonstrate what is possible; platforms and passports make it more repeatable.

---

### 6.4 It is backed by city and national policy

Amsterdam explicitly includes reuse in projects, circular design in new developments, sustainable renovation of municipal property, and reuse as standard in public space.[^amsterdam-circular] The Dutch national target of a fully circular economy by 2050 creates a broader policy setting in which reuse is not just a fringe design concern.[^gov-2050]

**Interpretation:** policy does not guarantee reuse, but it creates legitimacy and demand.

---

## 7. Important distinctions and caveats

### 7.1 “Circular” is not always “reclaimed-material reuse”

Many Dutch circular-construction sources combine **reuse, recycling, biobased materials, material passports, design for disassembly, and low-carbon construction**. This file focuses on reclaimed-material reuse, but some actors and projects are broader than reuse alone.

### 7.2 Recycling/downcycling remains a major issue

A high recycling rate does not necessarily mean high-value component reuse. The Dutch context often shows strong circular ambition but still struggles with downcycling and the economic/logistical barriers to true reuse.

### 7.3 Platform claims can be self-reported

Claims such as “largest platform,” “CO2 reduction,” “tons reused,” and “number of materials organized” are often reported by project actors. They are useful but should be treated as **project-reported evidence** unless independently audited.

### 7.4 The Dutch bubble is distributed

Unlike the Swiss Cirkla-centered ecosystem, the Dutch bubble is not organized around one umbrella network. It is distributed across Rotterdam design culture, Amsterdam policy and data infrastructure, national platforms, material marketplaces, and scattered high-quality case studies.
