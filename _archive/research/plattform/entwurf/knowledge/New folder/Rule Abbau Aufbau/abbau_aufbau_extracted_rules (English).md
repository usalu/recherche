# Abbau Aufbau — Extracted Reuse Rules by Category

Source PDF: **BBSR-Online-Publikation 61/2024, _Abbau Aufbau: Entwicklung eines Cradle-to-Cradle-Prozesses für Ortbetonelemente_**, including the appended **Handbuch zur Wiederverwendung von Stahlbetonelementen aus dem Rückbau von Gebäuden**.

**Method:** The rules below are extracted from explicit procedural, technical, legal, logistical and environmental guidance in the PDF. Each rule is preceded by the page reference and the concrete source evidence it is derived from. Page numbers refer to the PDF page numbers shown in the uploaded file.

**Rule format:**

- **Evidence:** page-specific factual basis from the PDF.
- **Rule:** operational instruction derived from that evidence.

---


## Structural


### S-001 — Select a structurally relevant donor building

**Evidence:** PDF p. 53: The guide says a donor building must be found and, for this project, should have as much of the raw structure as possible made from in-situ reinforced concrete.

**Rule:** Choose donor buildings whose reusable stock is predominantly in-situ reinforced concrete, preferably structures where slabs, beams, columns or walls can become structural elements again.


### S-002 — Secure demolition legality before structural planning

**Evidence:** PDF pp. 53–54: The source states that a demolition or deconstruction permit/notification must be obtained, and for attached buildings a qualified structural assessment of neighbouring stability is required.

**Rule:** Do not start reuse planning until the deconstruction of the donor building is legally possible and neighbouring structures can remain stable during removal.


### S-003 — Create a structural-system overview before cutting

**Evidence:** PDF p. 71: The structural engineer must inspect existing statics, formwork and reinforcement drawings and prepare load-path, span and slab-direction overviews.

**Rule:** Before drawing cut lines, document the donor building’s load-bearing system, span directions, supports, beams, walls, columns and reinforcement logic.


### S-004 — Let the structural engineer guide cut positions

**Evidence:** PDF p. 71: The guide says the structural engineer should provide notes on suitable cut positions and on elements that should preferably be removed whole.

**Rule:** Treat cut-line design as a structural decision; architectural cutting may proceed only with structural input on where separation is safe and useful.


### S-005 — Perform a first visual structural-quality screening

**Evidence:** PDF p. 75: The source requires a visual inspection of construction quality, deformations, damage, spalling, cracks, corrosion and repairs.

**Rule:** Reject or flag elements with visible defects until the damage mechanism, structural relevance and repairability are assessed.


### S-006 — Investigate cracks wider than 0.3 mm

**Evidence:** PDF p. 76: The source highlights cracks above 0.3 mm and significant spalling as points where reinforcement corrosion must be ruled out.

**Rule:** Any cracked or spalled region that could have exposed reinforcement must be checked for corrosion before reuse is accepted.


### S-007 — Use corrosion diagnostics where needed

**Evidence:** PDF pp. 76–77: The guide describes electrochemical potential measurement and partial exposure of reinforcement as methods to detect suspected corrosion.

**Rule:** When corrosion is suspected, use appropriate non-destructive or minimally destructive tests and record their results per element.


### S-008 — Measure carbonation depth

**Evidence:** PDF pp. 77–78: The source explains carbonation as loss of concrete alkalinity and describes the phenolphthalein test on core samples.

**Rule:** For structural reuse, determine whether carbonation has reached or threatens the reinforcement depth, especially for exposed or long-stored elements.


### S-009 — Test concrete material properties in a lab

**Evidence:** PDF p. 78: The guide lists chloride content, pollutants, compressive/tensile strength, E-modulus and density as properties to determine from cores.

**Rule:** Base structural capacity and durability assumptions on tested material parameters, not on visual inspection alone.


### S-010 — Validate rebound-hammer results

**Evidence:** PDF p. 79: The source notes that Schmidt-hammer rebound values can be affected by hardened carbonated surfaces and should be compared with core tests.

**Rule:** Use rebound testing only as a supplementary screening method; calibrate or verify it against destructive core strength tests.


### S-011 — Estimate remaining service life

**Evidence:** PDF p. 79: The guide says the test information should be used to prepare a prognosis for remaining service life and needed repair measures.

**Rule:** Approve an element only when its expected remaining service life matches the intended new use or can be achieved through repair.


### S-012 — Document reinforcement cover, diameter and spacing

**Evidence:** PDF p. 79: The source states that concrete cover, bar diameters and bar spacing must be investigated and compared with reinforcement plans where available.

**Rule:** Treat reinforcement geometry as mandatory reuse data for every load-bearing element.


### S-013 — Detect reinforcement when drawings are missing

**Evidence:** PDF p. 79: The guide says that if no reinforcement plans exist, reinforcement positions and bar diameters must be located and documented by detection methods.

**Rule:** Never assume reinforcement layout in undocumented donor elements; survey it before structural verification.


### S-014 — Choose shallow-rebar detection knowingly

**Evidence:** PDF pp. 80–82: The source describes magnetic/ferromagnetic methods and their limited penetration depth of about 70 mm.

**Rule:** Use magnetic methods mainly for cover and near-surface reinforcement; use other techniques when deeper reinforcement must be mapped.


### S-015 — Use radar or ultrasound for deeper reinforcement

**Evidence:** PDF pp. 82–83: The guide describes impulse radar and ultrasound echo methods for reinforcement deeper than about 70 mm.

**Rule:** Select radar, ultrasound or other higher-penetration methods when the structural proof depends on reinforcement deep inside the element.


### S-016 — Base the first cut plan on structural behaviour

**Evidence:** PDF p. 97: The guide asks whether there are construction joints, whether the raw structure is skeletal or massive, and what the engineer found about the static concept.

**Rule:** Start cut planning from the donor’s real structural logic, not from a purely geometric grid.


### S-017 — Respect standard transport geometry while cutting

**Evidence:** PDF pp. 97–100: The source states that elements should not exceed roughly 13 m length, 3 m height and 3 m width if special transports are to be avoided.

**Rule:** Cut structural elements into sizes that can be transported and handled without exceptional logistics unless the project deliberately budgets for special transport.


### S-018 — Use a consistent element raster

**Evidence:** PDF p. 100: The guide recommends defining a basic concept and, where possible, a specific element width so the new building can be based on a fixed grid.

**Rule:** Choose a repeatable element width or module early, so cut elements can form a coherent structural and architectural system in the new building.


### S-019 — Iterate cut plan and new plan together

**Evidence:** PDF p. 101: The source says elements from the first cut plan must be placed on the new floor-plan sketch and the cut or plan adjusted through several iterations.

**Rule:** Design the donor cut and the new building as one coupled system; revise both until structure, space and element inventory align.


### S-020 — Develop a connection concept early

**Evidence:** PDF p. 101: The guide says a concept must be developed for how used elements are rejoined, including possible new steel or concrete supports and demountability.

**Rule:** Do not treat reused elements as isolated pieces; define how they connect to one another and to new construction before detailed approval and tendering.


### S-021 — Check the cut plan structurally before execution

**Evidence:** PDF p. 123: The source states that the cut design must be checked by a structural engineer for stability during deconstruction and crane capacity.

**Rule:** A cut plan is not executable until the engineer confirms stable intermediate states and feasible lifting.


### S-022 — Ensure stability in every deconstruction state

**Evidence:** PDF p. 123: The guide says the deconstruction plan must guarantee stability in all construction states, including sufficient bracing.

**Rule:** Verify not only the original and final structures, but every temporary state created by cutting and removing elements.


### S-023 — Prefer top-down deconstruction

**Evidence:** PDF p. 123: The source states that deconstruction should generally proceed from top to bottom to avoid additional shoring.

**Rule:** Remove elements in a sequence that avoids creating unsupported loads below active work zones.


### S-024 — Brace before removing stabilising elements

**Evidence:** PDF pp. 123–125: The guide says removing a bracing wall can require temporary steel cross-bracing, and remaining slab parts may need temporary supports.

**Rule:** Provide temporary bracing or shoring whenever an extracted element contributes to stability, diaphragm action or load distribution.


### S-025 — Check crane capacity against real pick positions

**Evidence:** PDF p. 125: The source explains that mobile-crane capacity depends strongly on outreach, height, boom angle and position, with large variation by load position.

**Rule:** Limit element size and mass according to the specific crane, crane position, lifting radius and site constraints, not only by truck capacity.


### S-026 — Prove structural capacity and serviceability for approval

**Evidence:** PDF pp. 135–137: The ZiE/vBG criteria include load-bearing capacity and serviceability with structural and stability proof.

**Rule:** For reused structural elements, supply the same level of structural proof expected from conventional approved products or construction types.


### S-027 — Upgrade elements if required criteria are not met

**Evidence:** PDF p. 137: The guide states that reused elements must either already satisfy requirements or be improved by strengthening or other measures.

**Rule:** If an element fails a requirement, decide whether to repair, strengthen, reclassify for a lower-demand use or exclude it.


### S-028 — Move connection design into approval planning when needed

**Evidence:** PDF p. 138: The source notes that new connections may need to be planned already during approval because they overlap with regulatory criteria.

**Rule:** Plan structurally decisive connections early enough to support ZiE/vBG, fire, sound and stability proofs.


### S-029 — Obtain special approval for reused structural products or systems

**Evidence:** PDF pp. 144–145: The guide explains that reused reinforced-concrete elements need special approval such as vBG, ZiE or possibly a waiver, because normal approvals do not cover them.

**Rule:** Do not specify reused load-bearing concrete elements as if they were ordinary market products; route them through the required project-specific approval path.


### S-030 — Expect structural proof checking for special construction

**Evidence:** PDF p. 153: The source explains that reused reinforced concrete is a special construction type and can trigger checking even for otherwise lower building classes.

**Rule:** Assume that structural calculations for reused concrete elements will likely require formal checking unless the authority confirms otherwise.


### S-031 — Provide a complete stability proof

**Evidence:** PDF p. 151: The guide says buildings must be stable as a whole and in their parts and that structural system, drawings, calculations, fire resistance and ground conditions must be submitted.

**Rule:** The structural submission must include global stability, individual element capacity, fire resistance of load-bearing members, construction drawings and ground-bearing assumptions.


### S-032 — Focus the structural proof on connections

**Evidence:** PDF p. 152: The source says the main special focus is on connections between reused elements and their connection to new parts.

**Rule:** Treat interfaces between old-old and old-new components as critical proof points.


### S-033 — Assess fire resistance in the installed context

**Evidence:** PDF pp. 157–158: The guide states that reinforced concrete is non-combustible but that fire performance depends on element function, section, cover and installation with other materials.

**Rule:** Do not assign a blanket fire rating to reused concrete; calculate or justify it in its exact new support and connection context.


### S-034 — Let weak supports govern fire classification

**Evidence:** PDF p. 158: The source gives the example that a concrete beam on lower-rated steel brackets takes the brackets’ lower fire-resistance class.

**Rule:** Fire-protect connectors, brackets and supports so they do not reduce the fire rating of otherwise robust concrete elements.


### S-035 — Meet building-class fire and escape-route requirements

**Evidence:** PDF pp. 158–159: The guide says rescue routes, access and fire compartments must be planned according to building class just as in conventional construction.

**Rule:** Reuse of concrete does not relax fire-compartment, escape-route, stair, door, corridor or access requirements.


### S-036 — Detail individual connections for every adjoining part

**Evidence:** PDF pp. 205–206: The execution-planning table says foundations, vertical elements and horizontal elements need individual connection details to adjacent parts.

**Rule:** For every reused load-bearing element, produce details for how it connects to foundations, slabs, walls, columns, beams or roofs.


### S-037 — Add new bracing if reused elements cannot provide it

**Evidence:** PDF p. 206: The execution-planning table notes that new bracing elements may be required to stabilise reused concrete elements.

**Rule:** Do not assume recovered elements provide a complete stability system; add new bracing walls, frames or cores if required.


### S-038 — Plan service openings structurally

**Evidence:** PDF p. 207: The source notes that electrical and service installations may require core drillings or openings in reused concrete elements.

**Rule:** Coordinate penetrations with reinforcement maps and structural calculations before drilling or cutting reused elements.


### S-039 — Select connection types by interface

**Evidence:** PDF pp. 208–218: The guide illustrates anchors, stainless dowels, angle connectors, post-installed reinforcement with grout, steel holders and steel-beam bearings.

**Rule:** Choose connection hardware based on the specific interface: foundation–slab, slab–wall, slab–column, wall–slab, column–slab or slab-on-beam.


### S-040 — Fire-protect exposed steel angle connections

**Evidence:** PDF pp. 210, 212 and 216: The source repeatedly notes that angle connectors must be hidden by floor build-up or covered with fire-protection cladding.

**Rule:** Any steel connector that is part of the load path must be protected to the required fire-resistance level.


### S-041 — Recheck elements damaged in transport or storage

**Evidence:** PDF p. 262: The guide says if damage is noticed before installation, it must be assessed and repaired using the preliminary inspection methods.

**Rule:** A once-approved element must be re-evaluated if handling, transport or storage creates new cracks, spalling or other defects.


### S-042 — Verify supports during new structural work

**Evidence:** PDF pp. 259–262: The source lists checks for foundations, base slabs, vertical members, slabs and roofs, including supports, connectors, penetrations and joints.

**Rule:** During assembly, inspect each support and connection condition before the next load-bearing step proceeds.


### S-043 — Match earthworks and foundations to actual reused-element dimensions

**Evidence:** PDF p. 255: The guide says foundation pits or elements must match planned dimensions and the elements’ actual measurements.

**Rule:** Survey and reconcile actual element dimensions before finalising foundation and pit geometry.


### S-044 — Provide all foundation and slab connectors before placing elements

**Evidence:** PDF pp. 259–260: The guide lists needed reinforcement connectors, waterproofing, insulation and service penetrations at foundations and slabs.

**Rule:** Pre-install or accurately locate cast-in and post-installed connectors for reused walls, columns and slabs.


### S-045 — Check slab joints and joint connectors

**Evidence:** PDF p. 261: The source says that for panel floors, joint connectors must be executed according to the detailed design.

**Rule:** Treat joints between reused slab panels as structural details, not as simple filling gaps.


### S-046 — Check roof supports, penetrations and lightning protection

**Evidence:** PDF p. 262: The guide requires checking roof supports, roof build-up, penetrations for drainage/PV/solar thermal and lightning protection.

**Rule:** Where reused elements form roof structure, coordinate support proof, weathering layers, penetrations and lightning protection as one system.


### S-047 — Recognise tool limits for non-rectangular or wall-based structures

**Evidence:** PDF p. 32: The results state that the software focuses on rectangular-grid skeleton structures and is only partly usable for non-rectangular or wall-dominated buildings.

**Rule:** Do not rely on the Abbau-Aufbau tool as a universal structural cutter; validate its applicability to the donor building type.


### S-048 — Expect case-specific structural decisions

**Evidence:** PDF p. 32: The report says the diversity and unpredictability of existing constructions make universally applicable recommendations nearly impossible.

**Rule:** Use the handbook as a structured framework, but make structural decisions project by project with evidence from the specific building.


## Energy


### E-001 — Compare reuse and new-build cases

**Evidence:** PDF pp. 13–15: The report compares a ReUse case with a new-material reference case and considers modules A1–A4 for GWP and resource use.

**Rule:** Quantify benefits by modelling the same building once with reused concrete elements and once with equivalent new concrete elements.


### E-002 — Count used elements as already manufactured

**Evidence:** PDF pp. 13–14: The source assumes no new production emissions or primary-resource demand for reused concrete elements because they have completed one 50-year life cycle; only transport is counted.

**Rule:** For reused structural concrete elements, set A1–A3 manufacturing impacts to zero unless the project chooses a different allocation method; include transport and any project-specific processing separately.


### E-003 — Use a detailed quantity model

**Evidence:** PDF pp. 14–15: The report creates a detailed 3D model, reads volumes and areas, and links materials to LCA datasets through One Click LCA/Grasshopper.

**Rule:** Base LCA and resource calculations on a material quantity model rather than approximate gross-area assumptions.


### E-004 — Use recognised datasets for materials

**Evidence:** PDF p. 15: The source links each component to an EPD or Ökobaudat dataset and exports results for analysis.

**Rule:** Connect every quantified material to a transparent environmental dataset such as Ökobaudat or an EPD.


### E-005 — State the environmental system boundary

**Evidence:** PDF p. 15: The report fixes system boundaries to modules A1–A4 and excludes A5, B and C because they are considered equivalent for the compared scenarios.

**Rule:** Declare which life-cycle modules are included and justify exclusions before interpreting savings.


### E-006 — Use GWP as the primary climate metric

**Evidence:** PDF p. 15: The report focuses evaluation on Global Warming Potential in kg CO2-equivalents, while noting that other categories exist.

**Rule:** When communicating climate benefit, report GWP clearly and do not mix it with other indicators unless separately labelled.


### E-007 — Record primary-resource demand separately from CO2

**Evidence:** PDF pp. 15–16: The source calculates material intensity using Wuppertal Institute factors for abiotic, biotic, water and air resources.

**Rule:** Track primary-resource savings as a separate metric from CO2 because concrete reuse avoids both emissions and raw-material extraction.


### E-008 — Use the measured savings as a benchmark, not a guarantee

**Evidence:** PDF pp. 15–16 and 39: The report finds 36.15% GWP savings and 33.10% primary-resource savings for the studied model building.

**Rule:** Use the project’s 36% CO2 and 33% resource reductions as a reference benchmark, then calculate project-specific values.


### E-009 — Account for transport distance

**Evidence:** PDF p. 131: The guide states that longer transport worsens the LCA of reused elements and gives a truck transport factor of about 0.05 kg CO2-eq per tonne-kilometre.

**Rule:** Optimise source, storage and site locations so transport does not erode reuse benefits.


### E-010 — Do not use the theoretical transport break-even as a design target

**Evidence:** PDF pp. 131–132: The guide calculates that one tonne of reused concrete could theoretically travel about 3,400 km before matching new production emissions.

**Rule:** Treat long transport as technically possible but environmentally undesirable; still prioritise regional reuse.


### E-011 — Decide certification early

**Evidence:** PDF pp. 88–89: The guide says certification must be decided in an early project phase because criteria affect design and financing.

**Rule:** Make DGNB, BNB or QNG decisions before design is locked, so LCA and circularity requirements can shape the project.


### E-012 — Use reuse to support climate-certification criteria

**Evidence:** PDF pp. 91–92: The guide links reused reinforced concrete to DGNB climate and responsible-resource criteria and references the project’s 36% GWP reduction.

**Rule:** Use structural reuse evidence in sustainability certification, especially for GWP reduction and responsible resource sourcing.


### E-013 — Recognise that reuse is stronger than recycled aggregate for CO2

**Evidence:** PDF p. 17: The report says recycled concrete saves only about 2% CO2 in production compared with ordinary transport concrete because new cement is still needed.

**Rule:** Do not confuse recycling concrete into aggregate with reusing structural elements; the climate effect is much larger when cement and steel production are avoided.


### E-014 — Combine reuse with lower-carbon new concrete where new parts remain

**Evidence:** PDF pp. 17–18: The report notes that lower-carbon cement can additionally be applied to new parts such as foundations, beams and columns in the reuse scenario.

**Rule:** For unavoidable new concrete components, specify lower-carbon cement/concrete where feasible to stack reductions with reuse.


### E-015 — Consider timber where it outperforms reused concrete

**Evidence:** PDF p. 18: The report cites high GWP-saving potential for timber, especially in smaller buildings, and notes reused mineral construction may be more relevant for larger buildings.

**Rule:** Choose the structural material strategy by building type; reused concrete is not automatically better than timber for every scale.


### E-016 — Measure thermal performance if reused concrete is in the envelope

**Evidence:** PDF p. 163: The guide says U-value must be determined when reused concrete contacts exterior air or ground as part of the building envelope.

**Rule:** Do not place reused concrete in exterior walls, roofs or ground-contact assemblies without thermal calculation.


### E-017 — Determine concrete conductivity instead of assuming it blindly

**Evidence:** PDF p. 163: The source states that reinforced-concrete thermal conductivity depends on density, aggregates and reinforcement and must be determined according to DIN EN ISO 10456.

**Rule:** Use lab measurement or justified thermal-conductivity assumptions for reused concrete elements in the envelope.


### E-018 — Add enough insulation to meet GEG values

**Evidence:** PDF pp. 163–165: The example shows a 20 cm reused concrete wall requires about 14 cm wood-fibre insulation to reach U = 0.28 W/(m²K).

**Rule:** Size insulation from the actual reused element and target U-value, not from a generic wall type.


### E-019 — Develop an integrated energy concept

**Evidence:** PDF p. 166: The guide says GEG primary-energy demand must not exceed 55% of the reference building and may require both renewable supply and improved envelope performance.

**Rule:** Coordinate insulation, energy generation, heat supply, ventilation and envelope design in one energy strategy.


### E-020 — Prepare a full LCA according to DIN EN 15978

**Evidence:** PDF p. 175: The source says building LCAs are usually based on DIN EN 15978 and split the life cycle into phases A, B, C and D.

**Rule:** Structure the environmental assessment by recognised life-cycle modules instead of a single embodied-carbon number.


### E-021 — Create a material inventory for LCA

**Evidence:** PDF pp. 175–176: The guide says the LCA must begin with quantity take-off and that components must be grouped according to DIN 276.

**Rule:** Prepare an auditable quantity list of building elements, construction parts, products and materials before calculating impacts.


### E-022 — Match certification scope requirements

**Evidence:** PDF p. 176: The source states that DGNB certification requires all cost groups 300 and 400 to be assessed.

**Rule:** If LCA is used for certification, follow the certifier’s scope and level-of-detail requirements exactly.


### E-023 — Use Module D cautiously

**Evidence:** PDF p. 177: The guide says Module D is currently to be used only with reservation because end-of-life data is incomplete.

**Rule:** Report reuse, recovery and recycling benefits beyond the building life cycle transparently and separately.


### E-024 — Use a new-element reference to calculate avoided emissions

**Evidence:** PDF p. 179: The guide recommends creating a second LCA variant using new reinforced-concrete elements to calculate the GWP difference.

**Rule:** Always quantify avoided emissions against a clearly defined conventional substitute scenario.


### E-025 — Preserve environmental value by reversible fit-out

**Evidence:** PDF p. 267: The source recommends easy, non-destructive dismantling and exchangeability in interior works because interiors have shorter service lives.

**Rule:** Design the fit-out so future replacements do not destroy the reused structural system or create unnecessary waste.


### E-026 — Be transparent about LCA method limits

**Evidence:** PDF p. 18: The report notes that return-energy for deconstruction was not included and that the comparison is only material-level because a conventional building might have a more rational form.

**Rule:** State methodological limitations whenever presenting reuse savings.


## Semantic / Architectural


### A-001 — Use a circular vocabulary

**Evidence:** PDF p. 44: The foreword frames the shift as turning demolition into deconstruction and waste into material or elements.

**Rule:** Name and organise the project as resource recovery, not as demolition waste management.


### A-002 — Match donor and recipient projects

**Evidence:** PDF p. 62: Milestone 1 is the matchmaking of a deconstruction object with a new-build project, and either can come first.

**Rule:** Start with either a donor building or a new project, but do not proceed until a plausible Abbau–Aufbau match exists.


### A-003 — Define the new-building brief before matching elements

**Evidence:** PDF p. 57: The guide says the new project should have basic land-use rights clarified, a use concept and approximate area requirements.

**Rule:** Prepare a rough programme, site constraints and budget framework before designing with recovered elements.


### A-004 — Use funding and certification constraints as design inputs

**Evidence:** PDF pp. 57–59: The source discusses KfW, QNG and emerging circular-building support as early financing and planning issues.

**Rule:** Check funding, certification and sustainability requirements early enough to influence the design strategy.


### A-005 — Begin the new design with a rough floor-plan sketch

**Evidence:** PDF p. 97: The guide says a rough floor-plan sketch should be developed from the space programme and local planning constraints.

**Rule:** Use the spatial brief to create a first architectural receiving geometry before testing element placement.


### A-006 — Read the donor’s raw-structure geometry semantically

**Evidence:** PDF p. 97: The source asks whether the donor has construction joints, whether it is skeletal or massive, and whether slabs can be removed whole or are tied to columns/beams.

**Rule:** Classify each donor element by structural type and architectural reuse potential, not only by dimensions.


### A-007 — Choose an element concept

**Evidence:** PDF p. 100: The guide recommends a basic concept such as separate columns and slabs or paired columns with connecting beams.

**Rule:** Before detailed design, decide which semantic element types will drive the project: slabs, wall panels, column-slab pieces, frames, beams or hybrid fragments.


### A-008 — Use element width as an architectural grid

**Evidence:** PDF p. 100: The source recommends defining an element width so reuse in the new building can follow a fixed raster.

**Rule:** Translate recovered-element sizes into an architectural module for rooms, bays, facades and structural rhythm.


### A-009 — Iterate spatial design with the recovered inventory

**Evidence:** PDF p. 101: The guide says the new floor-plan sketch and the cut plan may both need changes over several iterations.

**Rule:** Let the inventory modify the architecture, and let architectural needs modify the cut strategy.


### A-010 — Create drawings of both cutting and remounting

**Evidence:** PDF p. 108: Milestone 3 requires not only typical new-building drawings but also representations of the cut and remounting concept.

**Rule:** Architectural deliverables must show where each element comes from and where/how it is reused.


### A-011 — Use certification criteria as a planning framework

**Evidence:** PDF pp. 89–90: The guide says certification can give designers and clients a framework for decisions on envelope, equipment, materials and standards.

**Rule:** When certification is chosen, translate criteria into project design rules rather than treating them as post-design documentation.


### A-012 — Do not assume circular construction is exempt from normal planning law

**Evidence:** PDF p. 141: The guide says the building code does not yet describe reuse cases, so conventional requirements apply to circular construction as well.

**Rule:** Design reused-element buildings to meet the same planning, fire, accessibility, sound and energy requirements as conventional buildings.


### A-013 — Check state-specific legal deviations

**Evidence:** PDF p. 141: The source says the Berlin-based guidance should be checked for deviations in other federal states.

**Rule:** When applying the rules outside Berlin, verify the local building-code, procedure and authority requirements.


### A-014 — Use regular approval for complex cases

**Evidence:** PDF p. 142: The guide warns that simplified procedures can miss public-law requirements and recommends deeper independent review or regular approval for large or complex projects.

**Rule:** For complex reused-structure projects, prefer a more robust approval pathway over a fast but risky simplified procedure.


### A-015 — Classify the building early

**Evidence:** PDF pp. 142–143: The source explains that fire and structural requirements depend on building class and special-building status.

**Rule:** Determine building class and special-building status before fixing structural and fire concepts.


### A-016 — Separate raw-structure planning from fit-out planning

**Evidence:** PDF p. 67: The guide distinguishes plans that show fit-out from plans that show the raw structure, noting that raw-structure plans are needed for reuse planning.

**Rule:** Model and draw the donor raw structure as a distinct planning object.


### A-017 — Model only what is reuse-relevant when preparing the structural donor model

**Evidence:** PDF p. 67: The source recommends a 3D model of the raw structure without fit-out because that is the structure used for cut planning.

**Rule:** Keep the donor model focused on reusable structural mass, reinforcement and interfaces rather than decorative or removable layers.


### A-018 — Use laser scanning only when it sees the relevant structure

**Evidence:** PDF p. 68: The guide says scanning is generally useful in the raw-structure state because fit-out can hide the structure relevant for reuse.

**Rule:** Do not spend high-resolution survey effort on surfaces that will be removed before structural reuse decisions are made.


### A-019 — Design openings and services around existing element conditions

**Evidence:** PDF p. 255: The guide notes that existing openings in reused elements may be used for service runs but then services must be placed very accurately.

**Rule:** Treat existing holes, edges and penetrations as design constraints and opportunities.


### A-020 — Use recovered geometry to inform roof and envelope form

**Evidence:** PDF p. 207: The execution-planning table notes that roof construction can depend on roof form, which may be influenced by reused elements.

**Rule:** Let the recovered structure influence the building form where this prevents wasteful recutting or excessive new support.


### A-021 — Make connection details legible to contractors

**Evidence:** PDF pp. 226–227: The guide recommends adding drawings and details to tender documents, especially for connections of used elements.

**Rule:** Architectural details must communicate unusual reuse assemblies clearly enough for pricing and construction.


### A-022 — Prefer contractors with reuse interest or experience

**Evidence:** PDF p. 231: The source suggests giving preference, where possible, to firms experienced with or interested in reused elements.

**Rule:** Select project partners who understand the experimental and circular nature of the construction task.


### A-023 — Estimate reused-element costs explicitly

**Evidence:** PDF pp. 101–106: The guide recommends a DIN 276 cost estimate and discusses using roughly 50% of comparable new-element costs as an initial allowance while separating deconstruction, transport and ownership incentives.

**Rule:** Include recovered elements as costed project components rather than assuming they are free.


### A-024 — Account for the visual and spatial consequences of reused elements

**Evidence:** PDF p. 101: The guide requires deciding where the new sketch deviates because of placed reused elements.

**Rule:** Accept that reuse may alter room proportions, grids, spans and alignments; record these as design decisions.


### A-025 — Use parametric tools as design assistance, not as design authority

**Evidence:** PDF pp. 19–23: The report describes the Grasshopper/Rhino tool for generating cut variants, inventories and IDs, while noting practical feedback and tool limits.

**Rule:** Use software to explore variants and inventories, but keep final decisions under architectural, structural and regulatory judgement.


### A-026 — Choose the appropriate cut-generation mode

**Evidence:** PDF pp. 21–22: The tool offers three modes: column mushrooms, frames and custom linework.

**Rule:** Select a cutting logic that matches the donor’s structural morphology rather than forcing all buildings into one pattern.


### A-027 — Represent available parts as a design library

**Evidence:** PDF pp. 22–23: The report says the tool creates 2D and 3D part libraries with IDs, dimensions, cut lengths, waste, volume, weight and grey energy.

**Rule:** Treat recovered elements as a catalogue of design parts that can be composed, tested and tracked.


### A-028 — Keep design recommendations adaptable

**Evidence:** PDF pp. 32–33: The report says practical examples are still missing and the handbook is general because experience with this construction method is limited.

**Rule:** Use the extracted rules as a design protocol that must be updated with project feedback and future pilot results.


## Logistics / Assembly


### L-001 — Create a binding component catalogue

**Evidence:** PDF p. 129: The guide says that after the cut plan is fixed, a binding catalogue with individual element identification must be created as the basis for logistics, storage and reinstallation.

**Rule:** No element should be cut, transported or installed without a catalogue entry and unique ID.


### L-002 — Include the required fields in the catalogue

**Evidence:** PDF p. 129: The source lists dimensions, opening dimensions, volume, mass and ID, and recommends sketches and test results where useful.

**Rule:** Each catalogue entry must identify geometry, weight, volume, openings, element type and any relevant test information.


### L-003 — Physically mark IDs before separation

**Evidence:** PDF p. 130: The guide says catalogue IDs should be transferred to the still-connected donor structure before cutting.

**Rule:** Mark every element before sawing so the physical piece remains linked to its digital and documentary record.


### L-004 — Use durable marking methods

**Evidence:** PDF p. 130: The source mentions waterproof marker, spray paint or attached plaques as possible marking methods.

**Rule:** Choose ID markings that remain readable through cutting, lifting, transport, storage and installation.


### L-005 — Use tracking and tracing when complexity warrants it

**Evidence:** PDF p. 130: The guide describes BIM, QR codes, online element databases and RFID chips for storing and transferring element information.

**Rule:** Apply QR/RFID/BIM tracking when there are many similar elements or different strengths, reinforcement layouts or installation positions.


### L-006 — Plan truck loads from catalogue and deconstruction order

**Evidence:** PDF p. 131: The source says transport combinations for low loaders should be developed from the catalogue and the deconstruction sequence.

**Rule:** Load plans must account for element order, weight, dimensions and the sequence in which elements leave the donor building.


### L-007 — Protect elements during transport

**Evidence:** PDF p. 131: The guide says load securing, element protection, and sometimes temporary bracing or supports must be considered.

**Rule:** Transport planning must prevent damage to edges, reinforcement zones, surface quality and geometry.


### L-008 — Choose storage close to both sites

**Evidence:** PDF p. 185: The source says the intermediate storage should not be too far from deconstruction and new-build sites because every kilometre worsens LCA.

**Rule:** Locate intermediate storage to minimise total tonne-kilometres and handling cycles.


### L-009 — Store in installation order

**Evidence:** PDF p. 185: The guide says the storage layout must allow elements to be removed according to the later installation sequence without costly restacking.

**Rule:** Arrange stacks so the next needed element is accessible when required.


### L-010 — Protect stored elements from weather

**Evidence:** PDF pp. 132 and 185–186: The source recommends covered storage or tarps because weather can cause corrosion, frost damage and accelerated carbonation.

**Rule:** Use roofed storage or weather protection whenever possible; if not, limit duration and cover elements.


### L-011 — Use protective spacers and correct orientation

**Evidence:** PDF p. 186: The guide recommends protective timbers between elements and storing elements in the same orientation as in the original building.

**Rule:** Store slabs lying flat, walls and columns standing where feasible, separated by protective supports.


### L-012 — Keep storage duration short

**Evidence:** PDF p. 132: The source says storage should be as short as possible and under the best possible conditions because interior concrete elements lack exterior-grade cover.

**Rule:** Design logistics to avoid long exposed waiting periods between removal and reuse.


### L-013 — Use selective deconstruction, not conventional demolition

**Evidence:** PDF p. 189: The guide states that reuse requires selective deconstruction instead of conventional demolition.

**Rule:** Plan the donor works as controlled disassembly and cutting, not crushing demolition.


### L-014 — Gut the building down to the raw structure

**Evidence:** PDF p. 190: The source says that because focus is on the raw structure, the donor building must first be stripped back to the reinforced-concrete skeleton.

**Rule:** Remove fit-out, services and non-structural materials before structural concrete cutting begins.


### L-015 — Separate waste streams

**Evidence:** PDF pp. 189–190: The guide lists elements and materials generated by selective deconstruction and states they must be separated under waste regulations.

**Rule:** Organise on-site separation for mineral waste, wood, gypsum, glass, metals, cables, plastics, hazardous materials and other streams.


### L-016 — Apply the waste hierarchy

**Evidence:** PDF pp. 190–191: The source says reuse, recycling and other recovery must be considered before final disposal.

**Rule:** For every removed material, test direct reuse first, then recycling/recovery, and use disposal only as the last option.


### L-017 — Document waste above the regulatory threshold

**Evidence:** PDF p. 191: The guide states that construction waste disposal from 10 m³ upward must be documented and shown to authorities on request.

**Rule:** Set up waste documentation before deconstruction begins, not after containers leave the site.


### L-018 — Use the relevant deconstruction standards

**Evidence:** PDF pp. 191–193: The source names ATV DIN 18459, DIN 18007 and VDI 6210 as relevant to planning, tendering and executing deconstruction.

**Rule:** Write the deconstruction tender and site process against the applicable demolition/deconstruction standards and guidelines.


### L-019 — Assign client planning and disposal responsibilities

**Evidence:** PDF pp. 193–194: The guide lists client tasks such as demolition/safety plans, permits, performance descriptions, award of services, contract details and disposal responsibility.

**Rule:** Clarify which client duties are retained and which are delegated to architects, engineers or contractors.


### L-020 — Ensure utilities are disconnected

**Evidence:** PDF p. 194: The source says the client must ensure media-free status by shutting off water, electricity, gas and telecommunications before deconstruction.

**Rule:** Confirm utility disconnection before heavy deconstruction and cutting work starts.


### L-021 — Commission a pollutant survey before tendering deconstruction

**Evidence:** PDF p. 194: The guide states that assessing the existing building for pollutants is a key prerequisite for planning and preparing the bill of quantities.

**Rule:** Do not tender or start deconstruction without knowing hazardous-material conditions.


### L-022 — Remove pollutants before structural deconstruction

**Evidence:** PDF p. 197: The source says pollutant removal and disposal should usually occur before the actual deconstruction works to meet occupational-safety measures.

**Rule:** Sequence hazardous-material removal before concrete cutting and lifting.


### L-023 — Prepare a deconstruction statics concept

**Evidence:** PDF p. 197: The guide defines demolition statics as preventing uncontrolled failure and says the deconstruction concept must define the sequence of cuts and removals.

**Rule:** Every structural cut and lift must be sequenced in a deconstruction concept that maintains safety.


### L-024 — Provide structural proof when removing load-bearing elements

**Evidence:** PDF pp. 197–198: The source says structural proof is required for deconstruction projects where load-bearing elements are changed or removed, except certain simpler cases.

**Rule:** Assume structural verification is necessary for extracting load-bearing concrete elements unless the project clearly qualifies for an exception.


### L-025 — Derive site setup and machine durations from the time plan

**Evidence:** PDF p. 198: The guide says the deconstruction concept can be used for a schedule, crane standing time, machine rental and site setup.

**Rule:** Plan equipment time, crane time and site facilities from the extraction sequence, not as generic allowances.


### L-026 — Provide deconstruction site equipment

**Evidence:** PDF pp. 198–199: The source lists site road, fence, scaffolding, fall protection, containers, toilet, office container, crane, hand tools and concrete saws.

**Rule:** Ensure all site access, safety, lifting, cutting, waste and welfare equipment is in place before selective deconstruction begins.


### L-027 — Specify concrete sawing accurately

**Evidence:** PDF p. 199: The guide says concrete-sawing descriptions should include cut lengths, cut depths, element to be cut, working heights, room heights and overhead work.

**Rule:** Concrete-cutting tenders must quantify geometry and working conditions sufficiently for pricing and safety planning.


### L-028 — Select sawing methods by depth and access

**Evidence:** PDF pp. 199–200: The source lists wall sawing, floor/joint sawing, wire sawing and chain sawing with different depth ranges.

**Rule:** Choose the cutting method according to element thickness, cut geometry, access, water/slurry control and reinforcement conditions.


### L-029 — Lift without damaging elements

**Evidence:** PDF p. 200: The guide permits chains, straps, bands or ropes and says auxiliary structures or holes may be needed for balanced load distribution.

**Rule:** Design lifting points and rigging so the element is stable in the air and not damaged by concentrated loads.


### L-030 — Follow lifting-safety rules

**Evidence:** PDF p. 200: The source cites operating-safety, workplace and DGUV/BG Bau rules for safe load attachment and lifting.

**Rule:** Only lift extracted concrete elements with compliant lifting gear, trained personnel and documented safe procedures.


### L-031 — Stay within regular transport thresholds where possible

**Evidence:** PDF pp. 200–201: The guide states that transport up to 2.55 m width, 4 m height, 18.75 m length and 44 t total weight needs no special permit under §32 StVZO.

**Rule:** Optimise element dimensions and load plans to avoid costly special transport, unless special permits are intentionally planned.


### L-032 — Integrate both transport legs into deconstruction tender

**Evidence:** PDF p. 201: The source recommends including transport from donor to storage and storage to new site in the deconstruction tender.

**Rule:** Use one responsible logistics scope for both transport stages where this reduces handling errors and damage risk.


### L-033 — Create a detailed construction and logistics schedule

**Evidence:** PDF p. 235: The guide says a precise construction schedule must be developed before actual works, especially as the basis for reused-element logistics.

**Rule:** Prepare an integrated Abbau–Aufbau schedule before site operations start.


### L-034 — Schedule both deconstruction and new construction

**Evidence:** PDF p. 235: The source says the time plan should show detailed works for deconstruction and new construction with agreed durations.

**Rule:** Coordinate donor extraction, storage, transport and new installation in one timeline.


### L-035 — Align the schedule with storage and installation sequence

**Evidence:** PDF p. 235: The guide says the schedule must be coordinated with the installation sequence established in intermediate-storage planning.

**Rule:** For every element, know when it leaves the donor, where it is stored, when it is transported again and when it is installed.


### L-036 — Share the current schedule with all parties

**Evidence:** PDF p. 235: The source recommends that all participants have the schedule and, if possible, use cloud-based tools for current access.

**Rule:** Maintain one live schedule visible to designers, deconstruction firms, transporters, storage managers and builders.


### L-037 — Get written schedule confirmation

**Evidence:** PDF pp. 235–236: The guide says the detailed schedule should be acknowledged in writing by contractors, otherwise delays cannot be warned against as agreed deadlines.

**Rule:** Turn schedule milestones into contractually acknowledged dates.


### L-038 — Balance just-in-time delivery with full truck loading

**Evidence:** PDF p. 236: The source says element deliveries should arrive at the right time and be optimally loaded to avoid unnecessary site storage, especially in dense urban sites.

**Rule:** Plan transport batches that are both efficiently loaded and aligned with immediate installation needs.


### L-039 — Submit the start-of-construction notice

**Evidence:** PDF pp. 237–238: The milestone says the authority must receive a start notice including site manager and confirmations for stability, fire, surveying and energy requirements.

**Rule:** Do not begin new-build works until the required start notice and prerequisites are submitted.


### L-040 — Apply for road closures and crane/container permits early

**Evidence:** PDF p. 238: The guide says street closures for crane positions, containers and similar uses may need at least two weeks, or longer when public transport is affected.

**Rule:** Secure public-space permits before they become critical path constraints.


### L-041 — Apply construction-site safety law during deconstruction

**Evidence:** PDF p. 241: The source lists occupational safety, construction-site, equipment-safety, workplace, noise/vibration, manual-handling and hazardous-substance regulations.

**Rule:** Treat selective deconstruction as high-risk construction work requiring formal occupational-safety management.


### L-042 — Appoint SiGeKo when multiple firms overlap

**Evidence:** PDF p. 241: The guide says a safety and health coordinator may be required when employees of several companies work simultaneously.

**Rule:** Coordinate safety across all firms when deconstruction, cutting, lifting and transport overlap.


### L-043 — Follow DGUV rules for contractor responsibilities

**Evidence:** PDF p. 242: The source states that construction companies must observe DGUV rules and protect employees on site.

**Rule:** Make each contractor responsible for its own statutory accident-prevention compliance while the client coordinates overall site safety.


### L-044 — Control vibration, noise, dust and water pollution

**Evidence:** PDF p. 242: The guide points to DIN 18007 appendices for requirements on vibration, noise, dust and possible water contamination during demolition/deconstruction.

**Rule:** Plan cutting and deconstruction methods with environmental and neighbour impacts controlled from the start.


### L-045 — Execute deconstruction in the correct order

**Evidence:** PDF p. 242: The source says the building is first gutted, then the reinforced-concrete raw structure is cut according to the cut planning using concrete-sawing methods.

**Rule:** Sequence works as strip-out first, structural cutting second, careful lifting/loading third.


### L-046 — Declare reuse purpose before removal

**Evidence:** PDF p. 243: The guide says to avoid reused concrete elements being treated as waste, the later use must be declared at the time of removal.

**Rule:** Create a project record showing that extracted elements are products for a defined new use, not waste.


### L-047 — Use a contract to prove element transfer and purpose

**Evidence:** PDF p. 243: The source recommends a contractual agreement between deconstruction client and new-build client specifying number, size, weight/volume and use purpose.

**Rule:** Document ownership transfer and intended reuse before elements leave the donor site.


### L-048 — Remark IDs during deconstruction if needed

**Evidence:** PDF p. 243: The guide says the architect should mark the catalogue identification numbers on the concrete elements during deconstruction.

**Rule:** Maintain physical traceability through every handling step, even if earlier markings are lost or covered.


### L-049 — Provide site access for heavy element delivery

**Evidence:** PDF p. 247: The source says the site entrance/road must allow element delivery to a position near the site where crane unloading and ideally direct placement are possible.

**Rule:** Design site access and unloading positions around the heaviest and least manoeuvrable reused elements.


### L-050 — Select crane type deliberately

**Evidence:** PDF p. 248: The guide says a crane is essential and mobile versus tower crane decisions must balance rental, operation and capacity; shared use must be clarified if contractor-provided.

**Rule:** Choose crane strategy as a central project decision, not as a late contractor convenience.


### L-051 — Provide basic site services and facilities

**Evidence:** PDF pp. 249–251: The guide lists waste containers, temporary power, temporary water, site office, toilets and lockable storage as site requirements.

**Rule:** Make the new-build site ready for simultaneous assembly, storage, safety and documentation work before elements arrive.


### L-052 — Reserve enough on-site storage area

**Evidence:** PDF p. 251: The source states that enough storage space must be available for construction materials and temporary storage of individual used concrete elements.

**Rule:** Even with just-in-time logistics, reserve contingency space for elements that cannot be installed immediately.


### L-053 — Verify openings and service routing in groundwork

**Evidence:** PDF p. 255: The guide notes that if reused elements contain openings used for services, lines must be laid very accurately to them.

**Rule:** Coordinate underground services and structural openings before casting or placing foundations and slabs.


### L-054 — Deliver used elements in installation sequence during structural works

**Evidence:** PDF p. 259: The source says the logistics/building sequence plan ensures reused elements arrive in the required order at the correct time for installation.

**Rule:** Tie transport dispatch directly to the day-by-day structural assembly sequence.


### L-055 — Repair damage before installation

**Evidence:** PDF p. 262: The guide says damage observed before installation must be assessed and reworked before reuse.

**Rule:** Do not install a damaged reused structural element until inspection confirms it is safe or repairs are complete.


### L-056 — Continue normal construction administration

**Evidence:** PDF pp. 262–263 and 267–268: The source says architects remain responsible for acceptance, invoice review, cost control and change-order management during structural and fit-out works.

**Rule:** Reuse does not remove ordinary site-supervision, acceptance, cost-control and claims-management duties.


### L-057 — Tender reused-element assembly as installation of client-supplied parts

**Evidence:** PDF pp. 224–226: The sample texts describe used concrete elements as client-provided and the contractor’s work as mounting them with required supports, reinforcement bars, connectors and grout.

**Rule:** Define in the tender exactly which parts are supplied by the client and which fastening, lifting, grouting and installation work the contractor prices.


### L-058 — Sequence tenders according to the works

**Evidence:** PDF p. 226: The guide says earthworks and services tenders come first, then structural works, then fit-out because the works occur in that order.

**Rule:** Prepare procurement in the same order as the physical dependency chain.


### L-059 — Give bidders equal deadlines

**Evidence:** PDF p. 226: The source recommends contacting all companies on the same day and giving the same tender deadline, usually at least ten working days.

**Rule:** Keep procurement transparent and fair, especially where circular construction is unfamiliar to contractors.

### V-010 — Record visual defect evidence

### V-011 — Record lab and field tests per element

**Evidence:** PDF pp. 78–79: The guide lists concrete and reinforcement tests whose results should be prepared for later structural calculation.

### V-012 — Use the component catalogue as the main evidence register

### V-014 — Use digital tracking for element properties
